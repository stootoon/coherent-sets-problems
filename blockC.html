Block C — The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

The paper's central result (Section 3) is that for $dx=Ax\,dt+\xi$, the subdominant singular functions are linear, $v_i^\top x$, with $v_i$ solving Eq. (18). This block derives it, explains why linear functions suffice, and probes the saddle-point case the paper's interpretation rests on.

Problem 8 — Moments and the Lyapunov equation

Setting $dx=Ax\,dt+\sqrt D\,dW$, $x\in\R^n$, $A$ real; $\bar x(t)=\E[x_t]$, $\Sigma(t)=\E[x_tx_t^\top]$ (for zero mean).

Goal: derive the paper's Eqs. (14)–(15) with an intuition for the Lyapunov equation, and see exactly what fails at a saddle.

(a) Derive $d\bar x/dt=A\bar x$ and $d\Sigma/dt=A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top+D$ via Itô. Conclude that for stable $A$ the stationary mean is zero and obtain Eq. (15).

Solution

Mean: take expectations of the SDE; the noise term has zero mean, so $d\bar x/dt=A\bar x$ and $\bar x(t)=e^{At}\bar x(0)\to0$ when all eigenvalues of $A$ have negative real part. Zero mean is forced by stationarity, not just preserved.

Covariance: Itô's product rule, $d(xx^\top)=(dx)x^\top+x(dx)^\top+(dx)(dx)^\top$ with $(dx)(dx)^\top=\sqrt D\,dW\,dW^\top\sqrt D^\top=D\,dt$: $$d(xx^\top)=(Axx^\top+xx^\top A^\top+D)\,dt+\text{(zero-mean martingale terms)}.$$ Taking expectations, $\dot\Sigma=A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top+D$. Stationarity $\dot\Sigma=0$ gives the Lyapunov equation $A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top=-D$, Eq. (15). Read it as a balance: the drift term contracts the covariance (for stable $A$), $D$ injects variance, and $\Sigma$ is where they cancel. Scalar case: $2a\sigma^2+d=0\Rightarrow\sigma^2=d/(2|a|)$.

(b) Verify that $\Sigma=\int_0^\infty e^{At}De^{A^\top t}\,dt$ solves Eq. (15) for stable $A$ and interpret it.

Solution

$A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top=\int_0^\infty\big(Ae^{At}De^{A^\top t}+e^{At}De^{A^\top t}A^\top\big)dt=\int_0^\infty\frac{d}{dt}\big(e^{At}De^{A^\top t}\big)dt=\big[e^{At}De^{A^\top t}\big]_0^\infty=0-D=-D$, using $e^{At}\to0$ for stable $A$.

Interpretation: the stationary state is $x_t=\int_{-\infty}^te^{A(t-s)}\sqrt D\,dW_s$, a sum of all past noise kicks, each propagated forward and shrunk by $e^{A(t-s)}$; the covariance adds up their (independent) contributions. Discrete-time analogue: the Kalman-filter prediction covariance fixed point $\Sigma=\Phi\Sigma\Phi^\top+Q$ with $\Phi=e^{A\Delta t}$. Uniqueness: the Sylvester equation $A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top=-D$ has a unique solution iff no two eigenvalues of $A$ sum to zero (Bhatia & Rosenthal 1997), which stable $A$ satisfies automatically.

(c) Show that $\rho_0(x)\propto\exp(-\tfrac12x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x)$ satisfies $L\rho_0=0$ when $\Sigma$ solves Eq. (15). Here $L$ is the Fokker–Planck operator of Problem 6 with OU drift, $Lp=-\nabla\!\cdot(Ax\,p)+\tfrac12\nabla\!\cdot(D\nabla p)$ (the paper's Eq. 11), which generates the evolution of densities, $\partial_t\rho=L\rho$; so $L\rho_0=0$ says $\rho_0$ is stationary, equivalently $\P_\tau\rho_0=\rho_0$ for all $\tau$.

Solution

$\nabla\rho_0=-\Sigma^{-1}x\,\rho_0$. Using the expansion from Problem 6(d), $$L\rho_0=-(\tr A)\rho_0-(Ax)\cdot\nabla\rho_0+\tfrac12\nabla\!\cdot(D\nabla\rho_0) =\rho_0\Big[-\tr A+x^\top A^\top\Sigma^{-1}x-\tfrac12\tr(D\Sigma^{-1})+\tfrac12x^\top\Sigma^{-1}D\Sigma^{-1}x\Big],$$ where the last two terms come from $\nabla\!\cdot(-D\Sigma^{-1}x\,\rho_0)=-\tr(D\Sigma^{-1})\rho_0+x^\top\Sigma^{-1}D\Sigma^{-1}x\,\rho_0$. Now use the Lyapunov equation to eliminate $D$: $\Sigma^{-1}D\Sigma^{-1}=-(\Sigma^{-1}A+A^\top\Sigma^{-1})$, so $\tfrac12x^\top\Sigma^{-1}D\Sigma^{-1}x=-x^\top A^\top\Sigma^{-1}x$ (the two quadratic forms are transposes of the same scalar), cancelling the second term; and $\tr(D\Sigma^{-1})=-\tr(A\Sigma\Sigma^{-1}+\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1})=-2\tr A$, so $-\tfrac12\tr(D\Sigma^{-1})=\tr A$, cancelling the first. Hence $L\rho_0=0$. (Alternatively: a linear SDE with Gaussian initial condition stays Gaussian, and (a) fixes its moments.)

(d) Take $A=\diag(-1,2)$, $D=I$. Solve the Sylvester equation, show $\Sigma$ is indefinite, and explain why Eq. (14) then defines no probability density.

Solution

Componentwise, $(A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top)_{ij}=(a_i+a_j)\Sigma_{ij}=-D_{ij}$. With $a=(-1,2)$: $\Sigma_{11}=-1/(2\cdot(-1))=\tfrac12$, $\Sigma_{22}=-1/(2\cdot2)=-\tfrac14$, $\Sigma_{12}\cdot(-1+2)=0\Rightarrow\Sigma_{12}=0$. So $\Sigma=\diag(\tfrac12,-\tfrac14)$, indefinite. Then $\rho_0\propto\exp(-x_1^2+2x_2^2)$ grows along the unstable direction and is not normalisable. The integral formula of (b) diverges for the same reason. What Eq. (14) gives at a saddle is a formal zero of $L$ (Kwon, Ao & Thouless 2005), describing the local shape of the density near the fixed point along stable directions and its growth rate along unstable ones — not a stationary distribution. Consequently $L^2(\rho_0)$, the adjoint of Problem 7, the contraction bound, and the compactness behind Eq. (7) are all unavailable as stated. This is the paper's open issue (i); its own suggested repair is to localise (window or condition on staying near the saddle), which would restore a normalisable reference measure. High that the gap exists; Medium on how much of the saddle interpretation survives a rigorous treatment.

(e) What goes wrong for $A=\diag(-1,+1)$, $D=I$?

Solution

Now $a_1+a_2=0$, so the $(1,2)$ equation reads $0\cdot\Sigma_{12}=0$: $\Sigma_{12}$ is undetermined and the Sylvester equation has a one-parameter family of solutions. This is precisely the excluded case "eigenvalues related by sign reversal" mentioned after Eq. (15). The symmetric saddle $\pm1$ is the natural first example one reaches for, and it is exactly the degenerate one; Problem 12 uses unequal rates for this reason.

Problem 9 — The reverse-time OU process

Setting Given (Anderson 1982; Haussmann & Pardoux 1986): the time reversal of a stationary diffusion $dx=b(x)\,dt+\sqrt D\,dW$ with stationary density $\rho_0$ is again a diffusion with the same $D$ and drift $b_{\rm rev}(x)=-b(x)+D\nabla\log\rho_0(x)$. This is the formula behind reverse-time SDEs in diffusion models.

Goal: identify the reversed OU explicitly, and get a clean matrix criterion for reversibility.

(a) Compute $\nabla\log\rho_0$ for the OU and show $A_{\rm rev}=\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}$, using the Lyapunov equation to eliminate $D$.

Solution

$\nabla\log\rho_0=-\Sigma^{-1}x$, so $b_{\rm rev}=-Ax-D\Sigma^{-1}x$. From $D=-(A\Sigma+\Sigma A^\top)$: $D\Sigma^{-1}=-A-\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}$, hence $$b_{\rm rev}(x)=-Ax+Ax+\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}x=\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}\,x.$$ The reversed process is linear with drift matrix $A_{\rm rev}=\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}$. Intuition: naïve reversal ($-A$) would run away from the fixed point; the score term $D\nabla\log\rho_0$ pulls back toward high stationary density; for a Gaussian the two combine into a linear drift that is $A^\top$ in $\Sigma$-adapted coordinates.

(b) Show the reversed process has the same $D$, the same stationary covariance $\Sigma$, and the same spectrum as $A$.

Solution

Same $D$ is part of the reversal theorem. Lyapunov for the reversed drift: $A_{\rm rev}\Sigma+\Sigma A_{\rm rev}^\top=\Sigma A^\top+A\Sigma=-D$, so $\Sigma$ solves the reversed Lyapunov equation too. $A_{\rm rev}$ is similar to $A^\top$, hence has the eigenvalues of $A$; in particular it is stable, and the reversed process is a legitimate stationary OU process. The reversed process is the one whose Koopman operator is $\K^\dagger_\tau$ (Problem 7d).

(c) Show $A_{\rm rev}=A$ iff $A\Sigma$ is symmetric, and that this is also the condition for $\tilde M=\Sigma^{-1/2}e^{A\tau}\Sigma^{1/2}$ to be symmetric.

Solution

$\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}=A\iff\Sigma A^\top=A\Sigma\iff(A\Sigma)^\top=A\Sigma$. For $\tilde M$: $\tilde M^\top=\Sigma^{1/2}e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1/2}$, and $\tilde M^\top=\tilde M\iff e^{A\tau}=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}=e^{\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}\tau}=e^{A_{\rm rev}\tau}$. If $A\Sigma$ is symmetric this holds for every $\tau$; conversely, differentiating at $\tau=0$ gives $A=A_{\rm rev}$. So $$\text{reversible}\iff A_{\rm rev}=A\iff A\Sigma=\Sigma A^\top\iff\tilde M=\tilde M^\top\iff\K^\dagger_\tau=\K_\tau .$$ The antisymmetric part of $A\Sigma$ is the probability current of the stationary state; its vanishing is detailed balance (Pavliotis 2014, §4.6). When it vanishes the singular vectors of $\tilde M$ are its eigenvectors, $u_i=v_i$, and the paper's predictive/retrospective distinction collapses. The distinction is therefore a claim about broken detailed balance in the stimulus dynamics.

(d) For $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\c&-2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=I$, solve for $\Sigma$, find the value of $c$ that makes the process reversible, and confirm the eigenvalues of $A$ are real for all $c$.

Solution

Write $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}&s_{12}\\s_{12}&s_{22}\end{pmatrix}$. Then $A\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}-s_{11}&-s_{12}\\cs_{11}-2s_{12}&cs_{12}-2s_{22}\end{pmatrix}$ and $A\Sigma+(A\Sigma)^\top=-I$ gives $-2s_{11}=-1$, $-3s_{12}+cs_{11}=0$, $2cs_{12}-4s_{22}=-1$, so $$s_{11}=\tfrac12,\qquad s_{12}=\tfrac c6,\qquad s_{22}=\tfrac{c^2}{12}+\tfrac14,\qquad A\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12&-\tfrac c6\\ \tfrac c6&-\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}.$$ $A\Sigma$ is symmetric iff $c=0$. So the process is reversible only when $A$ is diagonal (then $D=I$ and $A$ commute and $\Sigma$ is diagonal); any coupling $c\ne0$ makes it irreversible, with probability current $\propto c$. $A$ is lower-triangular, so its eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-2$ for every $c$, real and distinct, with real eigenvectors — the paper's standing assumption holds. This is the working example for Problems 12–13: a stable, real-spectrum OU for which $u_i\ne v_i$.

Problem 10 — Eq. (18) from the sibling kernel

Setting $F_\tau=\K_\tau\K^\dagger_\tau$ acts as $F_\tau f(s)=\int f(x)\,q(x\mid s)\,dx$ with $q(x\mid s)=\int p_\tau(y\mid s)\,p(x\mid y)\,dy$: go forward from $s$ to $y$, then Bayes-invert back to a "sibling" $x$ that could have produced the same $y$. Likewise $B_\tau=\K^\dagger_\tau\K_\tau$ goes backward then forward. All conditionals are Gaussian for OU.

Goal: derive both equations of Eq. (18) and the whitened form in the abstract.

(a) Show $\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+\tau})=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}$ and hence $\E[X_{t+\tau}\mid X_t=s]=e^{A\tau}s$, $\E[X_t\mid X_{t+\tau}=r]=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}r$.

Solution

Solving the SDE from $t$ to $t+\tau$: $X_{t+\tau}=e^{A\tau}X_t+\eta$ with $\eta=\int_t^{t+\tau}e^{A(t+\tau-s)}\sqrt D\,dW_s$ independent of $X_t$, $\operatorname{Cov}(\eta)=\Sigma_\tau=\int_0^\tau e^{At}De^{A^\top t}dt$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+\tau})=\E[X_tX_t^\top]e^{A^\top\tau}=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}$. Gaussian conditioning $\E[Y\mid X=s]=C_{YX}C_{XX}^{-1}s$: $$\E[X_{t+\tau}\mid X_t=s]=e^{A\tau}\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1}s=e^{A\tau}s,\qquad\E[X_t\mid X_{t+\tau}=r]=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\,\Sigma^{-1}r,$$ the second using $\operatorname{Cov}(X_{t+\tau})=\Sigma$ by stationarity. The backward conditional is linear regression of the past on the future.

(b) Show the backward mean map is the adjoint of the forward one in the inner product $\ip{a,b}_{\Sigma^{-1}}=a^\top\Sigma^{-1}b$, and equals $e^{A_{\rm rev}\tau}$.

Solution

$\ip{e^{A\tau}a,b}_{\Sigma^{-1}}=a^\top e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}b$ and $\ip{a,\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}b}_{\Sigma^{-1}}=a^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}b$ — equal. And $\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}=e^{\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}\tau}=e^{A_{\rm rev}\tau}$ (conjugation commutes with the exponential). So the backward mean is the mean of the reversed OU of Problem 9, and the $\Sigma^{-1}$-adjoint is the finite-dimensional shadow of the $\rho_0$-adjoint: on linear observables $v^\top x$, $\ip{v^\top x,w^\top x}_{\rho_0}=v^\top\Sigma w$, so adjointness in $L^2(\rho_0)$ restricted to linear functions is adjointness in the $\Sigma$ (dually $\Sigma^{-1}$) inner product. $\Sigma^{-1}$ is the Fisher metric of the Gaussian family; "backward = Fisher-adjoint of forward" is a coordinate-free way to say it.

(c) Compose the two means to get the mean $Ms$ of $q(\cdot\mid s)$, act on $f(x)=v^\top x$, and derive the first equation of (18). Repeat for $B_\tau$ to get the second.

Solution

$q$ is Gaussian in $x$ (a Gaussian mixture over $y$ of Gaussians with means linear in $y$, all of fixed covariance, is Gaussian), with mean $\E_q[x\mid s]=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}\E[X_{t+\tau}\mid X_t=s]=Ms$, $M=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}$. For linear $f$, $F_\tau f(s)=\E_q[v^\top x\mid s]=v^\top Ms$. The eigen-equation $F_\tau f=\lambda^2f$ becomes $v^\top M=\lambda^2v^\top$, i.e. $M^\top v=\lambda^2v$: $$e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma\;v=\lambda^2v,$$ the first equation of (18). For $B_\tau f(r)=\E\big[\E[f(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t]\mid X_{t+\tau}=r\big]$: inner mean $e^{A\tau}X_t$, outer $e^{A\tau}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}r=:Nr$, so $B_\tau(u^\top x)=u^\top Nx$ and $N^\top u=\lambda^2u$: $$\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\;u=\lambda^2u,$$ the second equation of (18). The covariance of $q$ never entered: it would only appear when $F_\tau$ acts on quadratic and higher observables, which is Problem 11.

The second equation by direct integration. With the forward Gaussian kernel $p_\tau(y\mid x)$, whose mean is $e^{A\tau}x$ (part (a)), and the backward kernel $\tilde p_\tau(x\mid r)$, whose mean is $\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}r$ (part (b)), $$B_\tau(u^\top x)(r)=\int\tilde p_\tau(x\mid r)\Big[\int p_\tau(y\mid x)\,u^\top y\,dy\Big]dx=\int\tilde p_\tau(x\mid r)\,u^\top e^{A\tau}x\,dx=u^\top e^{A\tau}\,\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}r=u^\top Nr .$$ Second equality: the integral of a linear function against a probability density is the linear function of the density's mean (linearity of the integral). Third: the same rule for the outer kernel, after taking the constant row vector $u^\top e^{A\tau}$ outside the integral. The covariances of both kernels never enter because only linear functions are integrated, which is the integral form of the remark that only the means matter here.

(d) Substitute $v=\Sigma^{-1/2}w$ to get $\tilde M^\top\tilde M\,w=\lambda^2w$ with $\tilde M=\Sigma^{-1/2}e^{A\tau}\Sigma^{1/2}$. Identify $\lambda_i$, and state precisely what "singular vector of the whitened transition matrix" means for $v_i$ and $u_i$. Show also that $\lambda_i\le1$ for stable $A$.

Solution

$\tilde M^\top\tilde M=\Sigma^{1/2}e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma^{1/2}$, so $M^\top=e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma=\Sigma^{-1/2}(\tilde M^\top\tilde M)\Sigma^{1/2}$. Hence $M^\top v=\lambda^2v\iff\tilde M^\top\tilde M(\Sigma^{1/2}v)=\lambda^2(\Sigma^{1/2}v)$: $w=\Sigma^{1/2}v$ is a right singular vector of $\tilde M$ and $\lambda$ the corresponding singular value. Similarly $N^\top=\Sigma^{-1/2}(\tilde M\tilde M^\top)\Sigma^{1/2}$, so $w'=\Sigma^{1/2}u$ is a left singular vector of $\tilde M$. In whitened coordinates $z=\Sigma^{-1/2}x$ (identity covariance), the singular functions are simply $v_i(x)=v_i^\top x=w_i^\top z$ and $u_i(x)=w_i'^\top z$: the projections of the whitened state onto the right and left singular vectors of the whitened one-step map $\tilde M$, which is the transition matrix in $z$-coordinates ($z_{t+\tau}=\tilde Mz_t+\text{noise}$). That is the abstract's statement.

Bound: by stationarity $\Sigma=e^{A\tau}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}+\Sigma_\tau$ (propagate the old covariance and add the new noise), so $\tilde M\tilde M^\top=\Sigma^{-1/2}(\Sigma-\Sigma_\tau)\Sigma^{-1/2}=I-\Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma^{-1/2}\preceq I$. All singular values are $\le1$, matching Problem 7(b), and $\lambda_i=1$ only along directions receiving no noise over the horizon. In Problem 14 the same $\tilde M$ appears as the canonical-correlation matrix between $x_t$ and $x_{t+\tau}$ and the $\lambda_i$ as canonical correlations.

Problem 11 — Why linear observables suffice

Setting 1-D reversible OU $dx=-ax\,dt+dW$ ($a>0$, $D=1$, $\sigma^2=1/(2a)$). Probabilists' Hermite polynomials $\mathrm{He}_n$ satisfy $\mathrm{He}_n''(\xi)-\xi\mathrm{He}_n'(\xi)+n\mathrm{He}_n(\xi)=0$. Script code/p11_hermite.py.

Goal: understand the sentence after Eq. (18) — "other eigenfunctions … represent higher-order polynomials … associated with less dominant eigenvalues" — and see which assumption it needs.

(a) Verify that $g_n(x)=\mathrm{He}_n(x/\sigma)$ is an eigenfunction of $L^\dagger=-ax\,\partial_x+\tfrac12\partial_x^2$ with eigenvalue $-na$, hence of $\K_\tau$ with eigenvalue $e^{-na\tau}$.

Solution

With $\xi=x/\sigma$: $g_n'=\mathrm{He}_n'/\sigma$, $g_n''=\mathrm{He}_n''/\sigma^2$, so $$L^\dagger g_n=-ax\frac{\mathrm{He}_n'}{\sigma}+\frac{\mathrm{He}_n''}{2\sigma^2}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[\mathrm{He}_n''-2a\sigma^2\,\xi\,\mathrm{He}_n'\Big]=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[\mathrm{He}_n''-\xi\mathrm{He}_n'\Big]=\frac{-n\,\mathrm{He}_n}{2\sigma^2}=-na\,g_n,$$ using $2a\sigma^2=1$. Then $\K_\tau g_n=e^{L^\dagger\tau}g_n=e^{-na\tau}g_n$. The eigenfunctions are orthogonal in $L^2(\rho_0)$ (Hermite polynomials are orthogonal under the Gaussian weight), $g_0=1$ is the trivial pair, and $g_1\propto x$ is the subdominant one with eigenvalue $e^{-a\tau}$, exactly $\tilde M=e^{-a\tau}$ for the 1-D case. Degree $n$ decays $n$ times faster. Deterministic-map intuition from Problem 0(e): monomials $x^n$ were eigenfunctions of $x\mapsto e^{-a\tau}x$; noise replaces them by Hermite polynomials (the monomials' Gaussian orthogonalisation) but keeps the eigenvalues $e^{-na\tau}$.

(b) For general stable $A$, show that $\K_\tau$, $\K^\dagger_\tau$ and hence $F_\tau$, $B_\tau$ map polynomials of degree $\le k$ into polynomials of degree $\le k$, and describe the resulting block-triangular structure.

Solution

$\K_\tau f(x)=\E[f(e^{A\tau}x+\eta)]$ with $\eta$ Gaussian independent of $x$. If $f$ is a polynomial of degree $\le k$, expanding $f(e^{A\tau}x+\eta)$ and taking the expectation over $\eta$ yields a polynomial in $x$ of degree $\le k$ (moments of $\eta$ are constants). The same holds for $\K^\dagger_\tau$, which is the Koopman operator of the reversed OU (Problem 9). So the flag of subspaces $\mathcal P_0\subset\mathcal P_1\subset\mathcal P_2\subset\cdots$ (polynomials of degree $\le k$) is invariant under $F_\tau$ and $B_\tau$; in a basis graded by degree the operators are block upper-triangular, and the spectrum is the union of the spectra of the diagonal blocks (degree-$k$ homogeneous parts modulo lower degree). The degree-$1$ block is exactly $M^\top$ (resp. $N^\top$) of Problem 10, with eigenvalues $\lambda_i^2$ = the squared singular values of $\tilde M$. In whitened coordinates the degree-$k$ block of $\K_\tau$ modulo lower degree is the symmetric tensor power $\mathrm{Sym}^k\tilde M$, so the degree-$k$ block of $F_\tau$ has eigenvalues equal to products $\lambda_{i_1}^2\cdots\lambda_{i_k}^2$. High for the invariant-flag statement; the spectrum of OU semigroups is worked out rigorously in Metafune, Pallara & Priola, J. Funct. Anal. 196 (2002). Polynomials are dense in $L^2(\rho_0)$, so nothing is missed.

(c) Conclude that the subdominant singular function of an attractive OU is linear, and state which assumption fails at a saddle.

Solution

For stable $A$ every $\lambda_i\le1$ (Problems 7b, 10d), so products of $k\ge2$ of them are $\le\lambda_1^{2k}\le\lambda_1^2$, with equality only in degenerate cases. The degree-$0$ block is the trivial eigenvalue $1$, the degree-$1$ block contains $\lambda_1^2$, and every higher block is smaller. Hence the non-trivial eigenfunction of $F_\tau$ with eigenvalue closest to one is $v_1^\top x$: linear. That is the paper's claim after Eq. (18).

At a saddle the argument breaks in two places. First, $\rho_0$ is not normalisable (Problem 8d), so there is no $L^2(\rho_0)$ in which to take adjoints, and $F_\tau$ is only formally defined. Second, even formally, the "singular values" of Eq. (18) straddle $1$ (Problem 12 finds $0.75$ and $1.04$ at $\tau=0.25$), so a degree-$2$ product $\lambda_1\lambda_2$ can be closer to one than either linear eigenvalue. The paper's rule "subdominant = closest to one" then does not obviously select a linear function. The Supplement (Sec. 3) states that it does not treat saddle points. Medium — a real gap, but one that the paper's proposed localisation might close.

(d) Numeric: discretise $L^\dagger$ on a grid for $a=1$ and confirm the spectrum and eigenfunctions.

Solution

Central differences on $[-7,7]$ with $801$ points: top eigenvalues of the discretised $L^\dagger$ are $0,-1,-2,-3,-4$ (to three decimals), and the corresponding eigenvectors have correlation $1.000$ with $\mathrm{He}_n(x/\sigma)$ on $|x|<3$. The discretisation is non-symmetric (as $L^\dagger$ is under Lebesgue measure) but has real spectrum, as it must: conjugating by $\rho_0^{1/2}$ makes it symmetric, which is the reversibility of 1-D OU.

import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.hermite_e import hermeval
a = 1.0; sigma = np.sqrt(1/(2*a))
N, L = 801, 7.0; x = np.linspace(-L, L, N); h = x[1]-x[0]
D1 = (np.eye(N,k=1)-np.eye(N,k=-1))/(2*h); D2 = (np.eye(N,k=1)-2*np.eye(N)+np.eye(N,k=-1))/h**2
Ldag = -a*np.diag(x)@D1 + 0.5*D2
w, V = np.linalg.eig(Ldag); idx = np.argsort(-w.real)[:5]
print(np.round(w[idx].real, 3))          # [ 0. -1. -2. -3. -4.]

Problem 12 — Saddle-point asymptotics (numeric)

Setting $A=S\,\diag(-a,b)\,S^{-1}$ with $a=1$, $b=\tfrac12$, stable eigenvector $e_s=(1,0)$, unstable eigenvector $e_u=(1,1)/\sqrt2$ (oblique), $D=I$. Script code/p12_saddle.py. (c) Explain the limits $\angle(v_1,e_s)\to90^\circ$ and $\angle(u_2,e_u)\to90^\circ$ (the Supplement Sec. 4 claim) via the rank-one asymptotics of $e^{A\tau}$.

Solution

For large $\tau$, $e^{A\tau}\approx e^{b\tau}e_u\ell_u^\top$ (dominant unstable mode; $\ell_u^\top e_u=1$). Then $$M^\top=e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma\approx e^{2b\tau}(e_u^\top\Sigma^{-1}e_u)\;\ell_u\,(\ell_u^\top\Sigma),$$ a rank-one matrix $c\,\ell_u\beta^\top$ with $\beta=\Sigma\ell_u$. Its non-zero eigenvector is $\ell_u$, so $v_1\to\ell_u\perp e_s$: the predictive filter becomes orthogonal to the stable subspace, i.e. it ignores the component of the state that will decay and reads out the coefficient of the unstable mode — the only thing that determines the far future. Similarly $$N^\top=\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\approx e^{2b\tau}(\ell_u^\top\Sigma\ell_u)\;(\Sigma^{-1}e_u)\,e_u^\top,$$ whose dominant eigenvector is $\Sigma^{-1}e_u$ and whose other eigenvector, for the small eigenvalue, must lie in the null space of the rank-one part: $e_u^\top u_2=0$, so $u_2\perp e_u$. The retrospective filter ignores the unstable component — which carries no information about where the trajectory came from, since everything gets amplified along $e_u$ — and reads out the stable component, whose present value is the decayed memory of the past. Left/right eigenvector biorthogonality of $A$ is doing all the work; $\Sigma$ only fixes the finite-$\tau$ corrections. High for the 2-D mechanism; the paper flags (open issue iii) that with several unstable directions the picture is not established.

(d) List every step of (a)–(c) that relied on $\Sigma$ being a covariance, and state what is formal rather than rigorous at the saddle.

Solution
  • Eq. (18) itself was derived (Problem 10) from Gaussian conditioning with $\Sigma=\operatorname{Cov}(X_t)$ and from the $\rho_0$-adjoint. With indefinite $\Sigma$, "$\E[X_t\mid X_{t+\tau}]$" under a non-normalisable $\rho_0$ has no probabilistic meaning; the matrices $M^\top$, $N^\top$ are the formal continuation of the stable-case formulas.
  • The interpretation of eigenvalues as $\lambda^2\le1$, of singular functions as members of an orthonormal basis, and of sign-thresholding as approximately optimal for Eq. (4) (Problem 5a) all used the contraction on $L^2(\rho_0)$ and the balance constraint under a probability measure. None applies.
  • Whitening ($\Sigma^{1/2}$) is unavailable; the reduction to an SVD of a real matrix is lost, and realness of the eigenvalues in (b) is an observed fact for this example, not a theorem.
  • What survives: the limits in (c) depend only on the eigenstructure of $A$, so the qualitative claim "predictive $\perp$ stable, retrospective $\perp$ unstable" is robust. What would make the intermediate-$\tau$ objects rigorous is a normalisable reference measure concentrated near the saddle — a windowed, discounted, or committor-conditioned process — which is exactly what the paper's open issue (i) proposes. Medium that such a construction reproduces Eq. (18) up to corrections; it has not been shown.
