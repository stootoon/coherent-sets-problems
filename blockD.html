Problem 13 — Galerkin projection: where Eq. (22) comes from

Setting A finite set of linearly independent features $\phi_1,\dots,\phi_d$ and their span $F=\{f=\vec c\cdot\vec\phi:\vec c\in\R^d\}$. A fixed inner product $\ip{\cdot,\cdot}$ on functions; from (b) on it is the stationary one, $\ip{f,g}=\int fg\,\rho_0=\E[f(X_t)g(X_t)]$. Matrices $[\Sigma_0]_{ij}=\ip{\phi_i,\phi_j}$ and $[\Sigma_\tau]_{ij}=\ip{\phi_i,\K_\tau\phi_j}$. Script code/p13_galerkin.py for (f).

Goal: understand "Galerkin projection" as a two-line recipe (apply the operator, then orthogonally project back), derive Eqs. (22)–(24) from it, and see the earlier blocks as special cases.

(a) The recipe. $\K_\tau$ acts on all functions of $x$, but a neuron can only represent elements of $F$. Explain why $\K_\tau f$ generally leaves $F$ even when $f\in F$. Then define the Galerkin approximation of $\K_\tau$ on $F$ as "apply $\K_\tau$, then take the closest element of $F$", write down the orthogonality condition this imposes, and derive Eq. (22), $K_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau$, as the matrix acting on coefficient vectors.

Solution

Take $f=\vec c\cdot\vec\phi\in F$. By linearity of $\K_\tau$ (Problem 1), $\K_\tau f=\sum_jc_j\,\K_\tau\phi_j$, and each $\K_\tau\phi_j(x)=\E[\phi_j(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t=x]$ is some new function of $x$ with no reason to be a combination of the $\phi_i$. Example: on a nonlinear system $\K_\tau x$ is a nonlinear function of $x$, so with the single feature $\phi(x)=x$ the image leaves $F$.

Step 1 (closest point = orthogonal projection). The closest element $g$ of a subspace $F$ to a given function $h$ is characterised by the residual $h-g$ being orthogonal to every element of $F$, equivalently to every basis function: $\ip{\phi_i,h-g}=0$ for all $i$. (Proof: for any $\delta\in F$, $\|h-g-\delta\|^2=\|h-g\|^2-2\ip{h-g,\delta}+\|\delta\|^2$, which is minimised at $\delta=0$ for all $\delta$ iff $\ip{h-g,\delta}=0$ for all $\delta\in F$.) This orthogonality condition is the Galerkin condition.

Step 2 (apply it to $h=\K_\tau f$). Seek $g=\vec c\,'\cdot\vec\phi$ with $\ip{\phi_i,\K_\tau f-g}=0$ for every $i$. Expanding both terms by linearity of the inner product, $$\sum_jc_j\ip{\phi_i,\K_\tau\phi_j}=\sum_jc'_j\ip{\phi_i,\phi_j}\qquad\text{for all }i,$$ which in matrix form is $\Sigma_\tau\vec c=\Sigma_0\,\vec c\,'$.

Step 3 (solve). Linear independence of the $\phi_i$ makes $\Sigma_0$ (a Gram matrix) invertible, so $\vec c\,'=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau\,\vec c$. The map $\vec c\mapsto\vec c\,'$ is the matrix $K_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau$, Eq. (22) (Supplement Eq. 51). Its $(i,j)$ entry answers: how much of $\phi_i$ appears in the projection of $\K_\tau\phi_j$.

The name comes from numerical PDEs, where Galerkin's method means: look for the solution in a finite-dimensional subspace and impose the equation only against test functions from that subspace. In the Koopman literature the same construction is called extended dynamic mode decomposition (EDMD; Williams, Kevrekidis & Rowley 2015, Klus et al. 2018), which are the paper's references for Eq. (22).

(b) Where the data enters. Nothing in (a) said which inner product to use. Show that with the stationary inner product $\ip{f,g}=\E[f(X_t)g(X_t)]$, the two matrices become $[\Sigma_0]_{ij}=\E[\phi_i(X_t)\phi_j(X_t)]$ and $[\Sigma_\tau]_{ij}=\E[\phi_i(X_t)\phi_j(X_{t+\tau})]$, and hence that Eq. (23) is their sample estimate. Which properties of the process does the estimate rely on?

Solution

$[\Sigma_0]_{ij}=\ip{\phi_i,\phi_j}=\E[\phi_i(X_t)\phi_j(X_t)]$ is the definition. For $\Sigma_\tau$, insert the definition of $\K_\tau$ and use the two rules of Problem 7(d): $$[\Sigma_\tau]_{ij}=\E\big[\phi_i(X_t)\,\K_\tau\phi_j(X_t)\big]=\E\Big[\phi_i(X_t)\,\E[\phi_j(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t]\Big]=\E\Big[\E\big[\phi_i(X_t)\phi_j(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t\big]\Big]=\E[\phi_i(X_t)\phi_j(X_{t+\tau})],$$ moving $\phi_i(X_t)$ inside the conditional expectation (it is a function of the conditioning variable) and then collapsing the nesting by the tower property. So $\Sigma_\tau$ is the lagged cross-covariance of the features, and $\Sigma_0$ their instantaneous covariance. Replacing each expectation by the time average $\tfrac1S\sum_s$ over a trajectory gives Eq. (23).

The estimate uses: stationarity, so that $X_{t_s}$ and the pairs $(X_{t_s},X_{t_s+\tau})$ have the same law for every $s$ and the averages estimate a single $\Sigma_0$, $\Sigma_\tau$; and ergodicity, so that a time average over one trajectory converges to the ensemble expectation. This is why the paper discusses local stationarity and exponential forgetting immediately after Eq. (23). It is also the reason for choosing the $\rho_0$ inner product rather than, say, Lebesgue: it is the one whose Gram matrices a neuron can estimate from the lagged correlations of its own inputs.

(c) Block A was Galerkin all along. On a finite chain with stationary distribution $\pi$, take the indicator features $\phi_i=\mathbf 1_{\{i\}}$, $i=1,\dots,N$. Compute $\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_\tau$ and show $K_\tau=P$. Why is there no approximation here? What does the same choice give for a continuous process and a partition of the state space?

Solution

$[\Sigma_0]_{ij}=\E[\mathbf 1_{\{i\}}(X_t)\mathbf 1_{\{j\}}(X_t)]=P(X_t=i,X_t=j)=\pi_i\delta_{ij}$, so $\Sigma_0=\diag(\pi)$. $[\Sigma_\tau]_{ij}=\E[\mathbf 1_{\{i\}}(X_t)\mathbf 1_{\{j\}}(X_{t+1})]=P(X_t=i,X_{t+1}=j)=\pi_iP_{ij}$, so $\Sigma_\tau=\diag(\pi)P$. Hence $K_\tau=\diag(\pi)^{-1}\diag(\pi)P=P$, the Koopman matrix of Problem 2(a). No approximation occurs because the indicators span every function on $N$ states, so $\K_\tau f$ never leaves $F$ and the projection is the identity. Note also that in coefficient coordinates the inner product is $\vec c_f^{\,\top}\Sigma_0\vec c_g=\sum_i\pi_ic_{f,i}c_{g,i}=\ip{f,g}_\pi$: the $\pi$-weighted inner product of Block A is what Supplement Eq. (50) becomes here.

For a continuous process and indicators of a partition $\{B_i\}$, the same computation gives $[\Sigma_0]_{ij}=\rho_0(B_i)\delta_{ij}$ and $[\Sigma_\tau]_{ij}=P(X_t\in B_i,X_{t+\tau}\in B_j)$, so $K_\tau$ is the matrix of transition probabilities between cells, $P(X_{t+\tau}\in B_j\mid X_t\in B_i)$: Ulam's method. Now there is an approximation (functions constant on cells are not invariant under $\K_\tau$), and the numerator of the paper's Eq. (4) is exactly one entry of this matrix.

(d) Linear features on OU are exact. Take the OU process of Block C and $\phi(x)=x$ (the paper's Eq. 26). Show that $F$ is invariant under $\K_\tau$, so the projection does nothing, and compute $K_\tau$ two ways: from the action of $\K_\tau$ on linear functions, and from Eq. (22) using $\Sigma_\tau=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}$ (Problem 10a). Explain the transpose.

Solution

For OU, $x_{t+\tau}=e^{A\tau}x_t+\eta$ with $\eta$ zero-mean Gaussian independent of $x_t$ (Problem 10a), so $\K_\tau(\vec c\cdot x)=\E[\vec c\cdot(e^{A\tau}x+\eta)]=\vec c\cdot e^{A\tau}x=(e^{A^\top\tau}\vec c)\cdot x$. The image is again linear, so $F=\{\vec c\cdot x\}$ is invariant, the Galerkin condition is satisfied with zero residual, and the representation is exact. Reading off the coefficient map: $K_\tau=e^{A^\top\tau}$.

From Eq. (22): $\Sigma_0=\E[x_tx_t^\top]=\Sigma$ and $\Sigma_\tau=\E[x_tx_{t+\tau}^\top]=\E[x_t(e^{A\tau}x_t+\eta)^\top]=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}$, so $K_\tau=\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}=e^{A^\top\tau}$. The two agree.

The transpose is not a convention clash. Points move by $x\mapsto e^{A\tau}x$; a linear observable is a covector $\vec c$, and covectors transform by the transpose (contragrediently): $\vec c\cdot(Mx)=(M^\top\vec c)\cdot x$. This is the same transpose that makes the paper's Eq. (18) read $e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma$ for the $v$'s rather than $e^{A\tau}\cdots$, and it is behind the naming trap noted on the overview page.

(e) The adjoint in coefficient coordinates, and Eq. (24). Show that for $f=\vec c_f\cdot\vec\phi$ and $g=\vec c_g\cdot\vec\phi$ the inner product is $\ip{f,g}=\vec c_f^{\,\top}\Sigma_0\,\vec c_g$ (Supplement Eq. 50). Deduce that the adjoint of the matrix $K_\tau$ with respect to this metric is $K_\tau^\dagger=\Sigma_0^{-1}K_\tau^\top\Sigma_0$, compare with Problem 3(a), and multiply out $K_\tau K_\tau^\dagger$ and $K_\tau^\dagger K_\tau$ to obtain Eq. (24).

Solution

By bilinearity, $\ip{f,g}=\sum_{ij}c_{f,i}c_{g,j}\ip{\phi_i,\phi_j}=\vec c_f^{\,\top}\Sigma_0\vec c_g$. So $\R^d$ carries the metric $\Sigma_0$, and the adjoint of a matrix $K$ is defined by $\vec c_g^{\,\top}\Sigma_0(K\vec c_f)=(K^\dagger\vec c_g)^\top\Sigma_0\vec c_f$ for all $\vec c_f,\vec c_g$. The left side is $\vec c_g^{\,\top}\Sigma_0K\vec c_f$ and the right side is $\vec c_g^{\,\top}K^{\dagger\top}\Sigma_0\vec c_f$; equating the matrices gives $\Sigma_0K=K^{\dagger\top}\Sigma_0$, i.e. $K^\dagger=\Sigma_0^{-1}K^\top\Sigma_0$. This is Problem 3(a) with $\diag(\pi)$ replaced by the Gram matrix $\Sigma_0$; (c) shows they coincide for indicator features. The paper writes this as "a direct consequence of Eq. (5)" just above Eq. (24).

With $K_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau$ and $\Sigma_\tau^\top=\Sigma_{-\tau}$ (the $(i,j)$ entry $\E[\phi_i(X_t)\phi_j(X_{t+\tau})]$ transposed is $\E[\phi_j(X_t)\phi_i(X_{t+\tau})]=\E[\phi_i(X_{t-\tau})\phi_j(X_t)]$ by stationarity): $K_\tau^\dagger=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau^\top\Sigma_0^{-\top}\Sigma_0=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_{-\tau}$, using $\Sigma_0^\top=\Sigma_0$. Then $$F_\tau=K_\tau K_\tau^\dagger=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_{-\tau},\qquad B_\tau=K_\tau^\dagger K_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_{-\tau}\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau,$$ which is Eq. (24). Their eigenvectors are the coefficient vectors of the singular functions (Eq. 25), because singular vectors of $K$ are eigenvectors of $KK^\dagger$ and $K^\dagger K$, exactly as in Problem 4. Problem 14 evaluates these for OU and identifies them with CCA.

(f) What projection loses (numeric). One-dimensional OU $dx=-x\,dt+\sqrt2\,dW$, stationary law $N(0,1)$, lag $\tau$. Using $x_{t+\tau}=e^{-\tau}x_t+\eta$ with $\eta\sim N(0,1-e^{-2\tau})$, compute $\K_\tau x^2$. (i) With features $\{x,x^2\}$, compute $\Sigma_0$, $\Sigma_\tau$ and $K_\tau$ analytically, and compare the $x^2$ entry of $K_\tau$ with the true coefficient $e^{-2\tau}$. Where did the discrepancy come from? (ii) Repeat with $\{1,x,x^2\}$. (iii) Confirm both with code/p13_galerkin.py, and relate the eigenvalues in (ii) to Problem 11.

Solution

$\K_\tau x^2=\E[(e^{-\tau}x+\eta)^2]=e^{-2\tau}x^2+(1-e^{-2\tau})$: a quadratic plus a constant. Also $\K_\tau x=e^{-\tau}x$ and $\K_\tau1=1$. Needed Gaussian moments: $\E[x^2]=1$, $\E[x^3]=0$, $\E[x^4]=3$.

(i) Features $\{x,x^2\}$. $\Sigma_0=\begin{pmatrix}\E[x^2]&\E[x^3]\\ \E[x^3]&\E[x^4]\end{pmatrix}=\diag(1,3)$. For $\Sigma_\tau$ use $[\Sigma_\tau]_{ij}=\E[\phi_i(x)\,\K_\tau\phi_j(x)]$: $\E[x\cdot e^{-\tau}x]=e^{-\tau}$; $\E[x\,(e^{-2\tau}x^2+1-e^{-2\tau})]=0$; $\E[x^2\cdot e^{-\tau}x]=0$; $\E[x^2(e^{-2\tau}x^2+1-e^{-2\tau})]=3e^{-2\tau}+1-e^{-2\tau}=1+2e^{-2\tau}$. So $$K_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau=\diag\!\Big(e^{-\tau},\ \tfrac{1+2e^{-2\tau}}{3}\Big).$$ The $x$ entry is exact. The $x^2$ entry is $\tfrac13+\tfrac23e^{-2\tau}$ instead of $e^{-2\tau}$: at $\tau=0.5$, $0.579$ versus $0.368$. The constant $(1-e^{-2\tau})$ in $\K_\tau x^2$ is not in $F$, and it is not orthogonal to $x^2$ either ($\ip{1,x^2}=1\ne0$), so the projection smears part of it onto the $x^2$ direction: coefficient $\ip{x^2,\,1-e^{-2\tau}}/\ip{x^2,x^2}=(1-e^{-2\tau})/3$, which is exactly the excess. A Galerkin matrix is only as good as the invariance of the feature span, and the error lands on whichever features are correlated with what was dropped.

(ii) Features $\{1,x,x^2\}$. Now $\K_\tau$ maps $F$ into itself (polynomials of degree $\le2$ are invariant, Problem 11), so the matrix is exact. Reading coefficients off $\K_\tau1=1$, $\K_\tau x=e^{-\tau}x$, $\K_\tau x^2=(1-e^{-2\tau})\cdot1+e^{-2\tau}x^2$, with column $j$ holding the coefficients of $\K_\tau\phi_j$: $$K_\tau=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1-e^{-2\tau}\\0&e^{-\tau}&0\\0&0&e^{-2\tau}\end{pmatrix},$$ upper triangular with eigenvalues $1,e^{-\tau},e^{-2\tau}$. Eq. (22) reproduces this (the reader may check: $\Sigma_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&3\end{pmatrix}$, $\Sigma_\tau=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&e^{-\tau}&0\\1&0&1+2e^{-2\tau}\end{pmatrix}$). The eigenvalues are those of the Hermite eigenfunctions $\mathrm{He}_0,\mathrm{He}_1,\mathrm{He}_2$ of Problem 11 (with $a=1$, $\sigma=1$), and the eigenvectors are their coefficient vectors: $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ and $(-1,0,1)$, i.e. $x^2-1=\mathrm{He}_2$. Including the constant feature is what makes the trivial pair $u_0=v_0=\mathbf 1$ of Problem 7(b) representable; the paper's Eq. (26) omits it, which is harmless for zero-mean linear features because $\ip{1,x}=0$, but would not be for $x^2$.

(iii) Running the script ($\Delta t=0.01$, $T=2\times10^4$, $\tau=0.5$) gives, for $\{x,x^2\}$, diagonal entries $0.601$ and $0.572$ against the analytic $0.607$ and $0.579$; for $\{1,x,x^2\}$, the $(1,3)$ entry $0.642$ against $0.632$ and eigenvalues $1.000,0.601,0.355$ against $1,0.607,0.368$. The residual gap is Euler–Maruyama bias at this $\Delta t$ (the discrete map has effective decay $1-\Delta t$ per step rather than $e^{-\Delta t}$), as in Problem 14(d).

Problem 14 — Past–future CCA, and how it recovers Eq. (18) (numeric)

Background: canonical correlation analysis. CCA (Hotelling 1936) takes two random vectors $X\in\R^p$ and $Y\in\R^q$ and asks for the pair of linear projections $a^\top X$, $b^\top Y$ that are most correlated; then for the next pair, uncorrelated with the first, and so on. The answer is the SVD of the whitened cross-covariance $\Sigma_{XX}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1/2}$: its singular values are the canonical correlations $1\ge\rho_1\ge\rho_2\ge\cdots\ge0$ and its singular vectors, unwhitened, are the canonical directions. Correlation is invariant under invertible linear maps of $X$ and of $Y$ separately, so CCA sees only the two subspaces spanned by the coordinates, not the coordinates themselves.

Past–future CCA (also time-lagged CCA) takes $X=\vec\phi(x_t)$ and $Y=\vec\phi(x_{t+\tau})$, the same features at two times. Stationarity makes $\Sigma_{XX}=\Sigma_{YY}=\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_{XY}=\Sigma_\tau$, so the whitened cross-covariance is $\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$, which is precisely the compressed Koopman operator of Problem 13 written in an orthonormal basis of $F$. Its reversible special case, where $\Sigma_\tau$ is symmetric and the two canonical bases coincide, is time-lagged independent component analysis (TICA; Molgedey & Schuster 1994), the standard tool of molecular kinetics; the general case is the VAMP framework of Wu & Noé (2020) and the "singular functions from data" of Koltai et al. (2018). In neuroscience the same object appears as predictive information (Bialek, Nemenman & Tishby 2001): for Gaussian processes the mutual information between past and future is $-\tfrac12\sum_i\log(1-\rho_i^2)$, so the canonical pairs are the coordinates in which predictive information decomposes, and the Gaussian information bottleneck (Chechik, Globerson, Tishby & Weiss 2005) keeps them in order of $\rho_i$. That is the equivalence with "information bottleneck and CCA" that the Supplement (Sec. 6) asserts without proof. Lipshutz, Bahroun, Golkar, Sengupta & Chklovskii (2021) give a biologically plausible online circuit for CCA, which is the paper's implicit answer to how a neuron could run this computation.

Setting The irreversible OU of Problem 9(d) with $c=1$, $D=I$; lag $\tau=0.5$. Features $\phi(x)=x$ (Eq. 26), so the Galerkin matrices of Problem 13 are $\Sigma_0=\E[x_tx_t^\top]$, $\Sigma_\tau=\E[x_tx_{t+\tau}^\top]$, $\Sigma_{-\tau}=\Sigma_\tau^\top$, and Eq. (24) reads $F_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_{-\tau}$, $B_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_{-\tau}\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau$. Script code/p14_cca.py.

Goal: see that a neuron with access only to lagged second moments of its input can, in principle, compute the singular vectors of Eq. (18).

(a) CCA from scratch. Maximise $a^\top\Sigma_{XY}b$ subject to $a^\top\Sigma_{XX}a=1$ and $b^\top\Sigma_{YY}b=1$. Derive the stationarity conditions, show the two Lagrange multipliers are equal to the achieved correlation $\rho$, eliminate $b$ to get $\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}\,a=\rho^2a$, and rewrite the whole problem as an ordinary SVD in whitened coordinates. Why is CCA unchanged if $X$ is replaced by $GX$ for invertible $G$, and what does that say about the choice of features in Problem 13?

Solution

Maximising correlation is maximising covariance at fixed variances, hence the constrained form. Lagrangian $\mathcal L=a^\top\Sigma_{XY}b-\tfrac\lambda2(a^\top\Sigma_{XX}a-1)-\tfrac\mu2(b^\top\Sigma_{YY}b-1)$. Setting $\partial_a\mathcal L=0$ and $\partial_b\mathcal L=0$: $$\Sigma_{XY}b=\lambda\,\Sigma_{XX}a,\qquad\Sigma_{YX}a=\mu\,\Sigma_{YY}b .$$ Left-multiply the first by $a^\top$ and the second by $b^\top$ and use the constraints: $a^\top\Sigma_{XY}b=\lambda$ and $b^\top\Sigma_{YX}a=\mu$. These are the same scalar (transpose of each other), so $\lambda=\mu=\rho$, the correlation achieved at the optimum. From the second equation $b=\rho^{-1}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}a$; substituting into the first, $\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}a=\rho^2\Sigma_{XX}a$, i.e. $\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}\,a=\rho^2a$, and symmetrically $\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}\Sigma_{XY}\,b=\rho^2b$. The top eigenvector gives the first canonical pair; the remaining eigenvectors give the successive pairs, and the constraint that later pairs be uncorrelated with earlier ones is the $\Sigma_{XX}$-orthogonality of eigenvectors of this (self-adjoint in the $\Sigma_{XX}$ metric, cf. Problem 3) matrix.

Whitened form. Put $\tilde a=\Sigma_{XX}^{1/2}a$, $\tilde b=\Sigma_{YY}^{1/2}b$ and $\tilde C=\Sigma_{XX}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1/2}$. The constraints become $\|\tilde a\|=\|\tilde b\|=1$, the objective $\tilde a^\top\tilde C\tilde b$, and the stationarity conditions $\tilde C\tilde b=\rho\tilde a$, $\tilde C^\top\tilde a=\rho\tilde b$: the singular value equations of $\tilde C$. So the canonical correlations are the singular values of $\tilde C$ and the canonical directions its singular vectors, unwhitened by $\Sigma^{-1/2}$. This is the same isometry trick as Problem 3(c): whitening turns a weighted problem into a Euclidean one.

Invariance. If $X\mapsto GX$ then $a^\top X=(G^{-\top}a)^\top(GX)$, so the set of achievable projections $\{a^\top X\}$ is unchanged and so are the optima and the $\rho_i$; only the coordinates of $a$ change. CCA therefore depends on $\operatorname{span}\{X_1,\dots,X_p\}$ alone. For Problem 13 this says the Galerkin matrices depend on the feature span $F$, not on the basis chosen for it, exactly as the derivation there (a projection onto $F$) implies; recombining the features by an invertible matrix changes $K_\tau$ by a similarity and leaves the singular functions untouched.

(b) Past–future specifics. With $X=\vec\phi(x_t)$, $Y=\vec\phi(x_{t+\tau})$ and the stationary inner product: (i) show that the whitened features $\vec\psi=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\vec\phi$ are orthonormal in $L^2(\rho_0)$ and that $\tilde C=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$ is the matrix of the compressed operator $C=P_F\K_\tau|_F$ in that basis; deduce $\rho_i\le1$ two ways. (ii) Show that reversibility makes $\Sigma_\tau$ symmetric, hence $a_i=\pm b_i$ (predictive and retrospective filters coincide) and CCA reduces to the generalised eigenproblem $\Sigma_\tau a=\rho\Sigma_0a$ of TICA. (iii) For jointly Gaussian $(X,Y)$ prove $I(X;Y)=-\tfrac12\sum_i\log(1-\rho_i^2)$, and evaluate it for the OU of part (d).

Solution

(i) $\E[\vec\psi\vec\psi^\top]=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\E[\vec\phi\vec\phi^\top]\Sigma_0^{-1/2}=I$, so $\ip{\psi_i,\psi_j}=\delta_{ij}$: an orthonormal basis of $F$. The matrix of $C$ in an orthonormal basis has entries $\ip{\psi_i,C\psi_j}=\ip{\psi_i,\K_\tau\psi_j}$ (the projection drops because $\psi_i\in F$) $=\E[\psi_i(x_t)\psi_j(x_{t+\tau})]$ by the tower-property step of Problem 13(b), and $\E[\vec\psi(x_t)\vec\psi(x_{t+\tau})^\top]=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1/2}=\tilde C$. So canonical correlations are the singular values of the compressed Koopman operator, and Problem 15(b) is the statement that CCA solves the two-sided restricted problem. Two proofs of $\rho_i\le1$: statistically, a correlation coefficient is at most one (Cauchy–Schwarz); operator-theoretically, $\sigma_i(C)\le\sigma_i(\K_\tau)\le1$ by Problem 15(a) and the contraction bound of Problem 7. That the two arguments agree is the content of the identification.

(ii) Detailed balance says $(x_t,x_{t+\tau})$ and $(x_{t+\tau},x_t)$ have the same joint law (Problem 7d), so $\E[\phi_i(x_t)\phi_j(x_{t+\tau})]=\E[\phi_j(x_t)\phi_i(x_{t+\tau})]$, i.e. $\Sigma_\tau=\Sigma_\tau^\top$ and $\tilde C$ is symmetric. The SVD of a symmetric matrix is its eigendecomposition up to signs ($\tilde C=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with $\sigma_i=|\lambda_i|$, $\tilde b_i=\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_i)\tilde a_i$), so $a_i=\pm b_i$: a reversible process has no distinction between predictive and retrospective filters, which is Problem 3(c)/9(c) again. The stationarity condition $\Sigma_{XY}b=\rho\Sigma_{XX}a$ with $a=b$ reads $\Sigma_\tau a=\rho\Sigma_0a$, the TICA generalised eigenproblem, whose solutions are the Galerkin eigenvectors of $K_\tau=\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_\tau$ themselves. For an irreversible process $\Sigma_\tau\ne\Sigma_\tau^\top$, eigenvectors of $K_\tau$ and singular vectors of $\tilde C$ part company, and one must use the latter: this is why the paper (and Problem 4) insists on singular rather than eigen-functions.

(iii) For a Gaussian vector with covariance $\Sigma$, $h=\tfrac12\log\det(2\pi e\Sigma)$, so $I(X;Y)=h(X)+h(Y)-h(X,Y)=\tfrac12\log\dfrac{\det\Sigma_{XX}\det\Sigma_{YY}}{\det\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{XX}&\Sigma_{XY}\\\Sigma_{YX}&\Sigma_{YY}\end{pmatrix}}$. Mutual information is invariant under invertible maps of each argument, so whiten: the joint covariance becomes $\begin{pmatrix}I&\tilde C\\\tilde C^\top&I\end{pmatrix}$, whose determinant by the Schur complement is $\det(I-\tilde C^\top\tilde C)=\prod_i(1-\rho_i^2)$, while the marginal determinants are $1$. Hence $I(X;Y)=-\tfrac12\sum_i\log(1-\rho_i^2)$: each canonical pair contributes independently, in decreasing order of $\rho_i$. For the OU process the lag vector is Gaussian, so this is exactly the predictive information between $x_t$ and $x_{t+\tau}$, and the $\lambda_i$ of Eq. (18) are its coordinates. With the analytic values of part (d), $\rho^2=(0.434,0.115)$: $I=-\tfrac12[\log0.566+\log0.885]=0.35$ nats, of which the subdominant pair carries $0.28$. The paper's "coherent set" filter is, for a Gaussian input, the single linear projection carrying the most predictive information; that is the sense in which the coherent-set, CCA and information-bottleneck pictures coincide, and the sense in which they do not: the information-theoretic statement needs Gaussianity, the CCA statement needs only second moments, and the coherent-set statement is about level sets of the singular function rather than the function itself.

(c) Simulate the process with Euler–Maruyama ($\Delta t=0.01$, $T=4000$, discard the transient) and estimate $\Sigma_0$, $\Sigma_\tau$.

Solution

Analytic values for reference: $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.1667\\0.1667&0.3333\end{pmatrix}$ and $A\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}-0.5&-0.1667\\0.1667&-0.5\end{pmatrix}$, not symmetric, so the process is irreversible and $F_\tau\ne B_\tau$ is expected. The estimator uses $k=\tau/\Delta t=50$ samples of lag; $\Sigma_0$ is estimated symmetrically from both ends of each lagged pair, $\Sigma_\tau$ from $X_0^\top X_1/n$.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm, solve_sylvester
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
A = np.array([[-1., 0.], [1., -2.]]); D = np.eye(2)
dt, T, tau = 0.01, 4000., 0.5; n = int(T/dt); k = round(tau/dt)
x = np.zeros((n, 2)); noise = rng.standard_normal((n, 2))*np.sqrt(dt)
for t in range(n-1): x[t+1] = x[t] + A @ x[t]*dt + noise[t]
x = x[n//10:]; X0, X1 = x[:-k], x[k:]
S0 = (X0.T@X0 + X1.T@X1)/(2*len(X0)); St = X0.T@X1/len(X0); Smt = St.T
F_hat = np.linalg.solve(S0, St) @ np.linalg.solve(S0, Smt)     # Eq. (24)
B_hat = np.linalg.solve(S0, Smt) @ np.linalg.solve(S0, St)

(d) Show analytically that Eq. (24) with linear features equals the matrices of Eq. (18), then compare eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the data estimates with the analytic ones.

Solution

From Problem 10(a), $\Sigma_\tau=\E[x_tx_{t+\tau}^\top]=\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}$ and $\Sigma_{-\tau}=e^{A\tau}\Sigma$, $\Sigma_0=\Sigma$. Substituting into Eq. (24): $$F_\tau=\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}\,\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma=e^{A^\top\tau}\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma=M^\top,\qquad B_\tau=\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}=\Sigma^{-1}e^{A\tau}\Sigma e^{A^\top\tau}=N^\top,$$ exactly the two matrices of Eq. (18). (This is the Supplement Sec. 6 claim; note it needs $\Sigma_0=\Sigma$, i.e. a stationary process with a genuine covariance — the same caveat as Problem 12(d).) Numerically, with $\tau=0.5$:

(e) Show that the eigenvectors of $F_\tau$ are the canonical directions of CCA between $x_t$ and $x_{t+\tau}$, and the eigenvalues the squared canonical correlations.

Solution

CCA between $X=x_t$ and $Y=x_{t+\tau}$ with $\Sigma_{XX}=\Sigma_{YY}=\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_{XY}=\Sigma_\tau$ seeks $a,b$ maximising $\operatorname{corr}(a^\top X,b^\top Y)$; the stationary conditions derived in (a) give the eigenproblems $\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}\,a=\rho^2a$ and $\Sigma_{YY}^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}\Sigma_{XX}^{-1}\Sigma_{XY}\,b=\rho^2b$, which are precisely $F_\tau a=\rho^2a$ and $B_\tau b=\rho^2b$ with $\rho$ the canonical correlations. Equivalently, the $\rho_i$ are the singular values of the whitened cross-covariance $\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$, whose right/left singular vectors are $\Sigma_0^{1/2}a$ and $\Sigma_0^{1/2}b$. Numerically: canonical correlations squared $=(0.438,0.123)=$ eigenvalues of $F_\tau$.

So on linear features the whole construction is past–future CCA (Koltai et al. 2018; Wu & Noé 2020, where $\sum\rho_i^2$ is the VAMP-2 score), and $\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_\tau\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$ is $\tilde M^\top$ of Problem 10(d): the whitened transition matrix is the canonical-correlation matrix. The paper's contribution beyond CCA is the coherent-set reading of the sign and the identification of the two canonical bases with predictive and retrospective units; the neural claim is that a cell can run this eigenproblem on its own lagged inputs, for which Lipshutz et al. (2021) give a local Hebbian/anti-Hebbian circuit. High for the CCA identity; the biological plausibility of the online estimator is outside this problem set.

Problem 15 — Is the approximation applied too early?

Background. Section 4 compresses $\K_\tau$ to a $d\times d$ matrix (Eq. 22), builds $F_\tau$ and $B_\tau$ from that matrix (Eq. 24), and takes their exact eigenvectors as the filters. A natural objection: what one presumably wants are approximate singular functions of the exact operator, suitably defined, and it is not obvious these coincide. In general they do not. For eigenvectors of $\K_\tau$, the compressed operator $C=P_F\K_\tau|_F$ can have eigenvalues nowhere near the spectrum of $\K_\tau$ ("spectral pollution"), and its eigenvectors need not approximate any eigenfunction; EDMD converges to $\K_\tau$ only in a weak sense that does not control individual eigenpairs (Korda & Mezić, J. Nonlinear Sci. 28, 2018). Residual DMD (Colbrook & Townsend, Comm. Pure Appl. Math. 77, 2024) exists precisely to repair this: it computes $\|\K_\tau f-\lambda f\|$ for each candidate pair and discards the spurious ones.

The paper is partly protected because it uses singular vectors, which obey a variational principle that eigenvectors of a non-normal operator do not: compressed singular values can never exceed the true ones, and the compressed pair is the exact solution of a well-posed restricted problem, "the most correlated present/future pair within the representable class" (the VAMP principle of Wu & Noé, J. Nonlinear Sci. 30, 2020). Two things remain. First, there is a hidden choice of where the projection sits: constraining both $f$ and $g$ to $F$ (the paper, and CCA) versus constraining only one of them (Rayleigh–Ritz for $\K_\tau\K^\dagger_\tau$), which give different answers. Second, the compressed pair equals the true one only when $F$ is invariant under $\K_\tau$ and $\K^\dagger_\tau$, which holds for OU with linear features (Supplement Sec. 6) but not for the nonlinear extension the paper proposes, and a neuron cannot measure the discrepancy from its own lagged covariances. This problem makes each of these statements precise.

Setting $\K=\K_\tau$ on $L^2(\rho_0)$ with adjoint $\K^\dagger$; a feature span $F$ with orthogonal projection $P_F$; the compressed operator $C=P_F\K|_F:F\to F$, whose matrix in coefficient coordinates is $K_\tau$ of Eq. (22). Singular values are written $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots$. For (c)–(e): the 1-D OU of Problem 13(f), $\K x^2=e^{-2\tau}x^2+(1-e^{-2\tau})$, $\ip{1,1}=1$, $\ip{x^2,x^2}=3$, $\ip{1,x^2}=1$. Script code/p15_residual.py.

Goal: know exactly in what sense exact singular vectors of the compressed operator are approximate singular functions of the exact one, where a modelling choice is hidden, and why the error is invisible to the estimator.

(a) Compression cannot inflate singular values. Using the Courant–Fischer characterisation $\sigma_i(\K)=\max_{\dim S=i}\min_{f\in S}\|\K f\|/\|f\|$, show $\sigma_i(C)\le\sigma_i(\K)$ for every $i$. Explain why no analogous statement holds for eigenvalues of a non-self-adjoint $\K$, and why Problem 13(f)(i) is nevertheless not a counterexample to (a).

Solution

For $S\subset F$, $\|Cf\|=\|P_F\K f\|\le\|\K f\|$ because an orthogonal projection is a contraction. Hence $$\sigma_i(C)=\max_{\substack{S\subset F\\\dim S=i}}\;\min_{f\in S}\frac{\|P_F\K f\|}{\|f\|}\;\le\;\max_{\substack{S\subset F\\\dim S=i}}\;\min_{f\in S}\frac{\|\K f\|}{\|f\|}\;\le\;\max_{\dim S=i}\;\min_{f\in S}\frac{\|\K f\|}{\|f\|}=\sigma_i(\K),$$ the first inequality by the contraction, the second by enlarging the set of subspaces maximised over. The same argument on $C^\dagger=P_F\K^\dagger|_F$ (the $\Sigma_0$-adjoint of Problem 13e) gives nothing new since $C$ and $C^\dagger$ share singular values.

Eigenvalues have no min–max characterisation unless the operator is self-adjoint (or normal). All one can say is that eigenvalues of $C$ lie in the numerical range $\{\ip{f,\K f}/\|f\|^2\}$ of $\K$, which for a non-normal operator can be much larger than the convex hull of its spectrum; that is spectral pollution. The 1-D OU of Problem 13(f) is reversible, so $\K$ is self-adjoint and positive, its singular values are its eigenvalues $e^{-n\tau}$, and the compressed value $0.579$ found there does obey (a): it sits below $\sigma_1=1$, and indeed between the true eigenvalues $0.607$ and $0.368$ whose eigenfunctions $x^2$ mixes (Cauchy interlacing). It is a spurious value in the sense that it approximates no eigenvalue, but not an inflated one. Pollution proper needs a non-normal $\K$, e.g. an irreversible chain or the OU of Problem 9(d).

(b) What the compressed pair optimises. Show that $$\sigma_1(C)=\max_{f,g\in F}\frac{\ip{g,\K f}}{\|f\|\,\|g\|},$$ attained at the top singular pair of $C$, and that the eigenvalues of the paper's $F_\tau$ are $\sigma_i(C)^2$. Conclude that the paper's filters are the exact solution of "the most correlated predictive/retrospective pair with both members representable", and state the VAMP-2 inequality $\sum_i\sigma_i(C)^2\le\sum_i\sigma_i(\K)^2$ with its equality case.

Solution

For $f,g\in F$, $\ip{g,\K f}=\ip{g,P_F\K f}=\ip{g,Cf}$ because $g\in F$ makes $\ip{g,(I-P_F)h}=0$ for any $h$. The maximum of $\ip{g,Cf}/(\|f\|\|g\|)$ over $F\times F$ is the top singular value of $C$, attained at its top singular pair (Cauchy–Schwarz in $g$ gives $\|Cf\|/\|f\|$, then maximise over $f$). By Problem 13(e), $F_\tau$ is the matrix of $CC^\dagger$, so its eigenvalues are $\sigma_i(C)^2$ and its eigenvectors the coefficient vectors of the $g$'s; $B_\tau$ likewise gives the $f$'s. In the language of Eq. (4)–(5), the paper's filters are the pair of representable observables whose present and future values are most correlated, which is exactly the CCA problem of Problem 14(a)–(b).

Summing (a) over $i\le d$: $\sum_{i\le d}\sigma_i(C)^2\le\sum_{i\le d}\sigma_i(\K)^2$, the VAMP-2 score bound. Equality holds iff $F$ is spanned by the top $d$ singular functions of $\K$ (then $C$ restricted to them is $\K$ itself, since both the input and the output lie in $F$). This turns feature choice into an optimisation with a known ceiling, and is the precise sense in which "approximating early" is harmless for singular vectors: the compressed problem is a lower bound on a variational objective of the exact operator, and maximising it over feature sets recovers the exact singular functions.

(c) The hidden choice. Rayleigh–Ritz applied directly to the self-adjoint operator $\K\K^\dagger$ would instead compute $\max_{g\in F}\|\K^\dagger g\|^2/\|g\|^2$, constraining only the retrospective function. Show this is $\ge\sigma_1(C)^2$. For the 1-D OU with the single feature $F=\operatorname{span}\{x^2\}$, compute both numbers at general $\tau$ and at $\tau=0.5$, identify the optimal $f$ in the one-sided problem, and say which of the two problems the paper is solving.

Solution

$\|\K^\dagger g\|=\max_{f}\ip{f,\K^\dagger g}/\|f\|=\max_f\ip{\K f,g}/\|f\|$ over all $f$, which is at least the maximum over $f\in F$; so $\max_{g\in F}\|\K^\dagger g\|/\|g\|\ge\sigma_1(C)$. The one-sided problem lets the predictive function leave $F$.

For $F=\operatorname{span}\{x^2\}$ the compressed operator is the scalar $c=\ip{x^2,\K x^2}/\ip{x^2,x^2}$. With $\K x^2=e^{-2\tau}x^2+(1-e^{-2\tau})$: $\ip{x^2,\K x^2}=3e^{-2\tau}+(1-e^{-2\tau})=1+2e^{-2\tau}$, so $c=\tfrac13(1+2e^{-2\tau})$ (as in Problem 13f) and $\sigma_1(C)^2=c^2$. The one-sided value (the process is reversible, $\K^\dagger=\K$) is $\|\K x^2\|^2/3$ with $\|\K x^2\|^2=e^{-4\tau}\cdot3+2e^{-2\tau}(1-e^{-2\tau})\cdot1+(1-e^{-2\tau})^2=1+2e^{-4\tau}$, i.e. $\tfrac13(1+2e^{-4\tau})$. At $\tau=0.5$: $c^2=0.335$ versus $0.424$; the true values $\sigma_i(\K)^2=e^{-2n\tau}$ are $1,\,0.368,\,0.135,\dots$ and both estimates obey their bounds. The gap is Jensen: $x^2=\mathrm{He}_0+\mathrm{He}_2$ has weights $\tfrac13,\tfrac23$ on eigenvalues $1,e^{-2\tau}$, and the one-sided value averages the squares $(1+2e^{-4\tau})/3$ while the two-sided one squares the average $((1+2e^{-2\tau})/3)^2$. The optimal $f$ in the one-sided problem is $\K^\dagger g/\|\K^\dagger g\|\propto\K x^2=e^{-2\tau}x^2+(1-e^{-2\tau})$, which contains a constant and is not in $F$.

The paper solves the two-sided problem: Eq. (24) is $CC^\dagger$, with the compression applied on both sides of the product. That is the right problem for a predictive and a retrospective neuron that each see only the features, and it is what CCA has always computed. But it is a modelling decision about representability, not an approximation to the one-sided Ritz problem, and neither is an approximation to the other. The paper does not flag the choice.

(d) When compression is exact, and when it is not. Show that if $F$ is invariant under both $\K$ and $\K^\dagger$ then $C$'s singular pairs are singular pairs of $\K$ with the same singular values. Verify the hypothesis for OU with linear features, using Problem 9. Then explain what the paper's construction returns for a nonlinear system with lag-vector features, and why a good VAMP score does not certify it.

Solution

If $\K F\subset F$ and $\K^\dagger F\subset F$ then $F^\perp$ is also invariant under both (for $h\perp F$ and $f\in F$: $\ip{f,\K h}=\ip{\K^\dagger f,h}=0$ since $\K^\dagger f\in F$; likewise for $\K^\dagger$). So $\K$ is block diagonal with respect to $F\oplus F^\perp$, $P_F\K|_F=\K|_F$ exactly, and $C^\dagger C=\K^\dagger\K|_F$; eigenvectors of $C^\dagger C$ are therefore eigenvectors of $\K^\dagger\K$, i.e. singular functions of $\K$, with singular values from the spectrum of $\K$ (a subset of it, the whole top block if $F$ contains the top singular functions).

OU with $\phi(x)=x$: $\K(\vec c\cdot x)=(e^{A^\top\tau}\vec c)\cdot x$ is linear (Problem 13d), and $\K^\dagger$ is the Koopman operator of the reversed process, which by Problem 9 is again OU with drift $A_{\rm rev}=\Sigma A^\top\Sigma^{-1}$, so $\K^\dagger(\vec c\cdot x)=(e^{A_{\rm rev}^\top\tau}\vec c)\cdot x$ is linear too. Both invariances hold, and Problem 14(d) is the resulting equality of Eq. (24) with Eq. (18). (Strictly, $F$ should include the constant so that $\lambda_0=1$ is represented; for zero-mean linear features $1\perp F$ and the block structure still holds.)

For a nonlinear process with lag-vector features $\phi(x)=x$, $\K x$ is a nonlinear function of the lag vector and neither invariance holds. The construction then returns the best linear filter pair: the linear predictive and retrospective observables most correlated across the lag. That is a well-defined and possibly useful object, but a coherent set of the underlying dynamics is a level set of a singular function which may be strongly nonlinear in the lag vector, and there is no theorem that the best linear pair approximates it. The VAMP score certifies only that $\sum\sigma_i(C)^2$ is as large as this feature class allows; without a reference value for $\sum\sigma_i(\K)^2$ it cannot say how far below the ceiling one is. Problem 13(f)(i) is the toy version: the feature set $\{x,x^2\}$ returns a confident $0.579$ where the truth is $0.368$, and nothing in the score flags it.

(e) Why the neuron cannot check (numeric). The natural certificate is the residual $r^2=\|\K f-\lambda g\|^2$ of a candidate pair. Expand it into three inner products and identify which ones are computable from $\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_\tau$. Show, via the law of total variance, that $\|\K f\|^2=\|f\|^2-\E\big[\operatorname{Var}(f(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t)\big]$, and that $\|\K f\|^2=\E[f(X_{t+\tau})f(X'_{t+\tau})]$ for two independent futures $X,X'$ of the same present. Explain why a single trajectory cannot supply this, evaluate everything for the pair of (c), and confirm with the script.

Solution

$r^2=\|\K f\|^2-2\lambda\ip{g,\K f}+\lambda^2\|g\|^2$. For $f,g\in F$ the last two terms are $\vec c_g^{\,\top}\Sigma_\tau\vec c_f$ and $\vec c_g^{\,\top}\Sigma_0\vec c_g$, both from Eq. (23). The first is $\ip{\K f,\K f}$, which is not a bilinear form in $\phi$ evaluated at two times, so no lagged second moment of the features gives it.

Law of total variance: $\E[f(X_{t+\tau})^2\mid X_t]=(\K f)(X_t)^2+\operatorname{Var}(f(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t)$. Taking expectations, $\|f\|^2=\|\K f\|^2+\E[\operatorname{Var}(f(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t)]$, using stationarity on the left. So the single-trajectory quantity one can estimate, $\E[f(X_{t+\tau})^2]$, overestimates $\|\K f\|^2$ by the mean conditional variance, and this is the same gap as between the one-sided and two-sided values in (c) (the contraction bound of Problem 7a is this inequality). For the sibling identity, condition on the present: $\E[f(X_{t+\tau})f(X'_{t+\tau})\mid X_t]=(\K f)(X_t)^2$ by conditional independence, and average. So $\|\K f\|^2=\ip{f,\K^\dagger\K f}$ is a two-trajectory quantity, the sibling kernel of Problem 10, and estimating it needs pairs of futures launched from the same present. A single time series offers each present exactly one future. In a deterministic system the conditional variance vanishes, $\|\K f\|^2=\|f\|^2$, and the residual is computable from one trajectory; that is why residual DMD works there and why its stochastic extension needs extra sampling.

For the pair of (c), $f=g=x^2/\sqrt3$, $\lambda=c$: $r^2=\|\K f\|^2-c^2=\tfrac13(1+2e^{-4\tau})-\tfrac19(1+2e^{-2\tau})^2=0.424-0.335=0.089$ at $\tau=0.5$, against $r^2=0$ for the true pair $(\mathrm{He}_2/\sqrt2,\,e^{-2\tau})$. The residual would expose the compressed pair, but only with $\|\K f\|^2$ in hand. The script estimates it two ways: from sibling futures (200 000 stationary presents, two Euler–Maruyama futures each) it finds $0.428$ against the analytic $0.424$, giving $r^2\approx0.10$; from the single-trajectory proxy $\E[f(X_{t+\tau})^2]/\|f\|^2=1.000$ it would report $r^2\approx0.67$, wrong by the mean conditional variance $0.576$. The neuron has the second kind of data, not the first.
