Block A — Everything as matrices

On a finite state space every operator is a matrix, every inner product is a weighted dot product, and the paper's Eqs. (4)–(9) can be computed by hand or in ten lines of NumPy.

The main result in three lines. Keep this in mind before any SVD is computed. $\K_\tau f(x)=\E[f(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t=x]$ takes a function of the future state (its input, the right side) and returns a function of the present state (its output, the left side). So in a singular pair $\K_\tau u_i=\lambda_iv_i$ the function $u_i$ lives on the future and $v_i$ on the present, and the adjoint $\K^\dagger_\tau v_i=\lambda_iu_i$ runs the other way, conditioning a present observable on the future state.

Why $\lambda\approx1$ means coherent. Take $u=\mathbf 1_B-\mathbf 1_{B^c}$, the $\pm1$ indicator of a future set. Then $\K_\tau u(x)=P(X_{t+\tau}\in B\mid X_t=x)-P(X_{t+\tau}\notin B\mid X_t=x)=2P(B\mid x)-1$. If some present set $A$ has $P(B\mid x)\approx1$ on $A$ and $\approx0$ on $A^c$, this is $\approx\mathbf 1_A-\mathbf 1_{A^c}=v$ with $\|v\|=\|u\|$: the pair $(u,v)$ is nearly a singular pair with $\lambda\approx1$, the sign of $v$ is the predictive set and the sign of $u$ the retrospective one.

Why nothing else can have $\lambda\approx1$. The law of total variance gives $\|\K_\tau u\|_{\rho_0}^2=\|u\|_{\rho_0}^2-\E\big[\operatorname{Var}(u(X_{t+\tau})\mid X_t)\big]$: conditional expectation shrinks a function by exactly the part of it that the present cannot predict (Problems 7a, 15e). So $\lambda=\|\K_\tau u\|/\|u\|$ is close to one precisely when $u(X_{t+\tau})$ is nearly determined by $X_t$. The subdominant pair is the most predictable non-constant observable of the future together with its best present-time predictor, and $1-\lambda_1$ measures what the noise takes away. With noise, $\K_\tau$ smooths, so the true singular functions are graded rather than $\pm1$-valued; taking their sign is the spectral-clustering step, and the region where $v_1$ is small is where membership is uncertain. Problem 4 computes all of this for a four-state chain, where you can watch it happen.

Problem 2 — Koopman and Perron–Frobenius on a finite chain

Setting $N$ states, transition matrix $P_{ij}=P(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i)$ (rows sum to one). Observables are column vectors $f\in\R^N$ ($f_i$ = value of the observable in state $i$); densities are row vectors $p$ with $\sum_ip_i=1$. Stationary distribution $\pi$ solves $\pi P=\pi$. For (b)–(d) use $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.2&0\\0.1&0.8&0.1\\0&0.2&0.8\end{pmatrix}.$$

Goal: see Eq. (4)'s numerator as a matrix computation and settle the direction in which $\K$ acts.

(a) Show that the Koopman operator acts on observables as $f\mapsto Pf$ and the Perron–Frobenius operator on densities as $p\mapsto pP$, and verify the duality $p\,(Pf)=(pP)\,f$.

Solution

$(Pf)_i=\sum_jP_{ij}f_j=\sum_jP(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i)f_j=\E[f(X_{t+1})\mid X_t=i]=(\K f)_i$. $(pP)_j=\sum_ip_iP_{ij}=P(X_{t+1}=j)$ when $X_t\sim p$, so $pP=\P p$. Duality is associativity of matrix multiplication: $p(Pf)=(pP)f=\sum_{ij}p_iP_{ij}f_j=\E[f(X_{t+1})]$. The two operators are the same matrix acting from the right and from the left.

(b) Take $B=\{3\}$. Compute $\K\mathbf 1_B$ entrywise and interpret each entry.

Solution

$\mathbf 1_B=(0,0,1)^\top$, so $\K\mathbf 1_B=P\mathbf 1_B=$ third column of $P$ $=(0,\,0.1,\,0.8)^\top$. Entry $i$ is $P(X_{t+1}\in B\mid X_t=i)$: the probability that a walker currently in state $i$ will be in $B$ one step later. This is a function on present states built from a set of future states — $\K$ pulls the indicator of $B$ back in time.

(c) Compute $\pi$. With $A=\{1,2\}$, verify that $\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\K\mathbf 1_B}_\pi/\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_A}_\pi=P(X_{t+1}\in B\mid X_t\in A)$, the numerator of the paper's Eq. (4). Then compute the "wrong-way" quantity $\ip{\mathbf 1_B,\K\mathbf 1_A}_\pi$ and say what it is.

Solution

$P$ is tridiagonal (a birth–death chain), so $\pi$ can be read off from the pairwise balance $\pi_iP_{i,i+1}=\pi_{i+1}P_{i+1,i}$: $0.2\pi_1=0.1\pi_2$ and $0.1\pi_2=0.2\pi_3$, giving $\pi\propto(1,2,1)$, i.e. $\pi=(0.25,0.5,0.25)$. (Check: $\pi P=\pi$.)

$\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\K\mathbf 1_B}_\pi=\sum_{i\in A}\pi_i(P\mathbf 1_B)_i=0.25\cdot0+0.5\cdot0.1=0.05$ and $\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_A}_\pi=\pi(A)=0.75$. The ratio is $0.05/0.75=1/15$. Directly: $P(X_{t+1}\in B\mid X_t\in A)=\sum_{i\in A}\pi_iP_{i3}/\pi(A)=(0+0.05)/0.75=1/15$. Agreed. In general $\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\K\mathbf 1_B}_\pi=P(X_t\in A,\,X_{t+1}\in B)$, the joint probability, and dividing by $\pi(A)$ conditions on the present set.

Wrong way: $\ip{\mathbf 1_B,\K\mathbf 1_A}_\pi=\pi_3(P\mathbf 1_A)_3=0.25\cdot(0+0.2)=0.05=P(X_t\in B,\,X_{t+1}\in A)$ — the joint probability of the reverse transition, $B$ now and $A$ next. It happens to equal the forward joint here because this chain is reversible (Problem 3d); the conditional probabilities differ: $0.05/\pi(B)=0.2\ne1/15$. For an irreversible chain even the joints differ.

Problem 3 — The weighted adjoint is the reversed chain

Setting Inner product $\ip{f,g}_\pi=\sum_i\pi_if_ig_i$ on observables, with $\pi$ stationary.

Goal: derive the paper's Eq. (16) in matrix form and see reversibility as the condition $P^\dagger=P$.

(a) From the defining relation $\ip{f,Pg}_\pi=\ip{P^\dagger f,g}_\pi$, derive $P^\dagger=\diag(\pi)^{-1}P^\top\diag(\pi)$.

Solution

$\ip{f,Pg}_\pi=\sum_i\pi_if_i\sum_jP_{ij}g_j=\sum_jg_j\Big[\sum_i\pi_iP_{ij}f_i\Big]=\sum_j\pi_jg_j\Big[\pi_j^{-1}\sum_i\pi_iP_{ij}f_i\Big]$. Comparing with $\ip{P^\dagger f,g}_\pi=\sum_j\pi_j(P^\dagger f)_jg_j$ gives $(P^\dagger f)_j=\pi_j^{-1}\sum_iP_{ij}\pi_if_i$, i.e. $$P^\dagger_{ji}=\frac{\pi_iP_{ij}}{\pi_j},\qquad P^\dagger=\diag(\pi)^{-1}P^\top\diag(\pi).$$ With $\pi\to\rho_0$ and $P^\top\to$ the Perron–Frobenius operator $e^{L\tau}$ (matrices act on row vectors from the right, so $P^\top$ acting on column vectors is $\P$), this is exactly the paper's Eq. (16).

(b) Show $P^\dagger_{ji}=P(X_t=i\mid X_{t+1}=j)$, so that $P^\dagger$ is the Koopman matrix of the time-reversed chain.

Solution

Bayes' rule with the stationary marginals: $P(X_t=i\mid X_{t+1}=j)=\dfrac{P(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i)P(X_t=i)}{P(X_{t+1}=j)}=\dfrac{P_{ij}\pi_i}{\pi_j}=P^\dagger_{ji}$. Hence $(P^\dagger f)_j=\sum_iP(X_t=i\mid X_{t+1}=j)f_i=\E[f(X_t)\mid X_{t+1}=j]$: $P^\dagger$ conditions backward on the future exactly as $P$ conditions forward on the past. The reversed chain $\tilde X_s=X_{-s}$ is Markov with transition matrix $P^\dagger$ and the same stationary distribution. Reading Eq. (16) as "weight by the prior, push forward, divide by the marginal" is reading Bayes' rule in operator form.

(c) Show $P^\dagger=P$ iff detailed balance $\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}$ holds, and that in that case $\tilde P=\diag(\pi)^{1/2}P\diag(\pi)^{-1/2}$ is symmetric.

Solution

$P^\dagger=P\iff\pi_iP_{ij}/\pi_j=P_{ji}$ for all $i,j\iff\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}$. Under detailed balance, $\tilde P_{ij}=\pi_i^{1/2}P_{ij}\pi_j^{-1/2}$ and $\tilde P_{ji}=\pi_j^{1/2}P_{ji}\pi_i^{-1/2}=\pi_j^{1/2}\,\dfrac{\pi_iP_{ij}}{\pi_j}\,\pi_i^{-1/2}=\pi_i^{1/2}P_{ij}\pi_j^{-1/2}=\tilde P_{ij}$.

The map $f\mapsto\diag(\pi)^{1/2}f$ is an isometry from $\ell^2(\pi)$ to plain $\ell^2$, and $\tilde P$ is $P$ transported through it. So "self-adjoint in the $\pi$-weighted inner product" is literally "symmetric after whitening by $\pi^{1/2}$". A reversible chain has real eigenvalues and orthogonal ($\pi$-weighted) eigenvectors; an irreversible one need not, which is why the paper works with singular functions of $\tilde P$ instead of eigenfunctions of $P$.

(d) Compute $P^\dagger$ for the chain of Problem 2 and decide whether it is reversible.

Solution

Every birth–death chain is reversible: the balance relations used to find $\pi$ in Problem 2(c) are detailed balance, and the only nonzero off-diagonal entries are nearest-neighbour. Check one entry: $P^\dagger_{21}=\pi_1P_{12}/\pi_2=0.25\cdot0.2/0.5=0.1=P_{21}$. So $P^\dagger=P$, and the forward- and backward-conditioned expectations coincide. This is why the wrong-way joint in Problem 2(c) came out equal to the right-way one.

Problem 4 — Coherent sets of a 4-state chain (numeric)

Setting Script code/p04_finite_chain.py. The "whitened Koopman matrix" is $\tilde P=\diag(\pi)^{1/2}P\diag(\pi)^{-1/2}$. Its SVD $\tilde P=U\Sigma V^\top$ gives $\tilde P\,V_{:,i}=\sigma_iU_{:,i}$; undoing the whitening, the paper's singular pair is $u_i=\diag(\pi)^{-1/2}V_{:,i}$ (future, retrospective) and $v_i=\diag(\pi)^{-1/2}U_{:,i}$ (present, predictive), satisfying $Pu_i=\sigma_iv_i$.

Goal: run Eqs. (6)–(9) on a concrete chain, then break reversibility and watch the common-future and common-past partitions come apart.

(a) Build the chain with two weakly coupled pairs $\{1,2\}$, $\{3,4\}$, $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.90&0.09&0.01&0\\0.09&0.90&0&0.01\\0.01&0&0.90&0.09\\0&0.01&0.09&0.90\end{pmatrix},$$ compute $\pi$ and $\tilde P$.

Solution

$P$ is symmetric and doubly stochastic, so $\pi=(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$ and $\tilde P=P$: whitening is trivial for a uniform stationary distribution. Detailed balance holds trivially ($\pi_iP_{ij}=P_{ij}/4=P_{ji}/4$).

(b) Compute the SVD of $\tilde P$; identify the trivial pair and the subdominant pair; sign-threshold the subdominant singular vectors.

Solution

Output: singular values $(1,\;0.98,\;0.82,\;0.80)$. The value $1$ belongs to the constant vector — the trivial pair $u_0=v_0=\mathbf 1$ of Eq. (7). The subdominant pair has $\sigma_1=0.98$ with $$v_1\propto u_1\propto(-1,-1,+1,+1),$$ so $\operatorname{sign}v_1$ partitions the states into $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$: Eq. (9) recovers the two weakly coupled pairs. Note the spectral gap $0.98\to0.82$: one non-trivial coherent pair, then fast-mixing modes within the pairs.

(c) Explain why the left and right subdominant singular vectors coincide here, and relate $\sigma_1$ to the leakage between the pairs. Evaluate the objective of Eq. (4) on $A=B=\{1,2\}$.

Solution

$\tilde P$ is symmetric (reversible chain, Problem 3c), so $\tilde P^\top\tilde P=\tilde P\tilde P^\top=\tilde P^2$ and the SVD is the eigendecomposition: $u_i=v_i$. Predictive and retrospective sets are the same — there is no arrow of time to distinguish "common future" from "common past".

Leakage: $Pv_1$ in row 1 gives $0.90+0.09-0.01=0.98$, so $\sigma_1=1-2\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon=0.01$ the per-step probability of leaving one's pair. The objective of Eq. (4) on $A=B=\{1,2\}$ is $P(\text{stay in }\{1,2\})+P(\text{stay in }\{3,4\})=0.99+0.99=1.98=1+\sigma_1$. Problem 5(a) shows this is not a coincidence: the objective equals $1+$ (a normalised bilinear form whose maximum is $\sigma_1$).

(d) Now take a directed ring $1\to2\to3\to4\to1$ with unequal probabilities of staying put, $\text{stay}=(0.1,0.3,0.1,0.3)$ and $P_{i,i+1}=1-\text{stay}_i$. Check that detailed balance fails, compute the subdominant pair, and interpret the two sign patterns.

Solution

Output: $\pi=(0.219,0.281,0.219,0.281)$, $\max_{ij}|\pi_iP_{ij}-\pi_jP_{ji}|=0.197$ (detailed balance badly violated — the walker only ever moves clockwise), singular values $(1,\;0.919,\;0.719,\;0.60)$, and $$v_1\propto(+0.88,\,+1,\,-0.88,\,-1),\qquad u_1\propto(-0.88,\,+1,\,+0.88,\,-1).$$ Sign patterns: $\operatorname{sign}v_1$ gives $V^\pm=\{1,2\}\mid\{3,4\}$, while $\operatorname{sign}u_1$ gives $U^\pm=\{2,3\}\mid\{4,1\}$. The partitions differ by one step around the ring, which is exactly what a coherent pair should do: mass in $V^+=\{1,2\}$ at time $t$ is (mostly) in $U^+=\{2,3\}$ at time $t+1$, since state 1 moves to 2 and state 2 moves to 3 with high probability. $v_1$ groups present states by common future; $u_1$ groups future states by common past; $U^+$ is the image of $V^+$ under the flow, as stated after Eq. (9).

The objective of Eq. (4) on the rounded pair $(\{1,2\},\{2,3\})$ is $1.9125$, close to the relaxation bound $1+\sigma_1=1.919$; the same-set pair $(\{1,2\},\{1,2\})$ scores only $1.21$. Predictive and retrospective neurons in the paper's language would here be a unit that fires for present states $\{1,2\}$ and a unit that fires for future states $\{2,3\}$: two different filters for the two halves of one coherent pair.

(e) Rounding is a heuristic. Repeat (d) with $\text{stay}=(0.7,0.9,0.7,0.9)$ (weak flow). Compare the objective of the sign-rounded pair with that of the same-set pair.

Solution

Output: $v_1\propto(-0.19,-1,+0.19,+1)$, $u_1\propto(+0.19,-1,-0.19,+1)$. The sign patterns again give $(\{1,2\},\{2,3\})$, but now the entries for states 1 and 3 are small — they sit near the decision boundary. The rounded pair scores $1.65$, the same-set pair $(\{1,2\},\{1,2\})$ scores $1.85$, and the relaxation bound is $1.92$. So sign-thresholding the singular vectors does not always return the best indicator pair; when the flow is weak the singular vectors say "states 2 and 4 are the cores, 1 and 3 are ambiguous", and hard rounding throws that information away. This is the motivation for the paper's soft (ReLU) reading in Section 5.2: the magnitude of the singular function carries membership confidence. High that rounding is only guaranteed up to Cheeger-type bounds (Froyland 2013, Sec. 4).

Problem 5 — Relaxation, and the spectral-clustering correspondence

Setting General stationary process with Koopman operator $\K$ (write $\K$ for $\K_\tau$), marginals $\mu=\nu=\rho_0$, and the objective of Eq. (4) with the balance constraint $\rho_0(A)=\rho_0(B)=p$.

Goal: see exactly in what sense the subdominant singular pair "solves" Eq. (4), and see normalised spectral clustering as the reversible special case.

(a) Let $J=\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\K\mathbf 1_B}_{\rho_0}$. Show that the objective of Eq. (4) equals $1+\dfrac{\ip{f,\K g}_{\rho_0}}{\|f\|_{\rho_0}\|g\|_{\rho_0}}$ with the centred indicators $f=\mathbf 1_A-p$, $g=\mathbf 1_B-p$, and hence that its maximum over all $f,g$ orthogonal to the constants is the subdominant singular value $\lambda_1$, attained at $(f,g)=(v_1,u_1)$.

Solution

Using $\K\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1$, $\ip{\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1}=p$ and $\ip{\mathbf 1,\K\mathbf 1_B}=\rho_0(B)=p$ (stationarity): $$\ip{f,\K g}=J-p\cdot p-p\cdot p+p^2=J-p^2,\qquad\|f\|^2=\|g\|^2=p-p^2=p(1-p).$$ For the complements, $\ip{\mathbf 1_{A^c},\K\mathbf 1_{B^c}}=\ip{\mathbf 1-\mathbf 1_A,\K(\mathbf 1-\mathbf 1_B)}=1-2p+J$. So the objective is $$\frac{J}{p}+\frac{1-2p+J}{1-p}=1+\frac{J-p^2}{p(1-p)}=1+\frac{\ip{f,\K g}}{\|f\|\,\|g\|}.$$ The second term is a normalised bilinear form of $\K$ restricted to the orthogonal complement of the constants (both $f$ and $g$ have zero mean). By the variational characterisation of singular values, $\max\ip{f,\K g}/(\|f\|\|g\|)$ over that complement is the largest singular value of $\K$ orthogonal to the trivial pair, namely $\lambda_1$, attained at $f=v_1$, $g=u_1$. Hence the objective is at most $1+\lambda_1$ for any balanced indicator pair, with the singular pair as the relaxed maximiser — the content of the paper's "well-approximated by certain singular functions". (The paper's $\lambda_i^2$ are the eigenvalues of $F_\tau=\K\K^\dagger$; the singular values of $\K$ itself are $\lambda_i$.) Check against Problem 4(c): $1+0.98=1.98$.

Two remarks. The constraint $\rho_0(A)=\rho_0(B)$ is what makes $f$ and $g$ have the same norm and the same centring; without it the algebra does not close. And the relaxation drops the requirement that $f,g$ take two values, which is why the rounding step in Problem 4(e) can lose.

(b) Let $W$ be a symmetric affinity matrix on $N$ vertices with degrees $d_i=\sum_jW_{ij}$, $D=\diag(d)$. Show that $P=D^{-1}W$ is the Koopman (transition) matrix of the random walk on the graph, that $\pi_i=d_i/\sum_kd_k$, and that the walk is reversible.

Solution

$P_{ij}=W_{ij}/d_i\ge0$ with $\sum_jP_{ij}=1$: from vertex $i$, jump to $j$ with probability proportional to the edge weight. With $\pi_i=d_i/Z$, $Z=\sum_kd_k$: $(\pi P)_j=\sum_i\frac{d_i}{Z}\frac{W_{ij}}{d_i}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_iW_{ij}=\frac{d_j}{Z}=\pi_j$ using $W=W^\top$. Detailed balance: $\pi_iP_{ij}=\frac{W_{ij}}{Z}=\pi_jP_{ji}$ by symmetry of $W$. So every undirected graph defines a reversible chain.

(c) Show that the second eigenvector of $D^{-1}W$ — the Fiedler vector of normalised spectral clustering — is the subdominant singular function in the sense of Problem 4, and explain when and why the SVD becomes necessary.

Solution

Whitening by $\pi^{1/2}\propto D^{1/2}$: $\tilde P=D^{1/2}(D^{-1}W)D^{-1/2}=D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}=I-L_{\rm sym}$, the symmetric normalised Laplacian's complement. $\tilde P$ is symmetric, so its SVD is its eigendecomposition (up to the sign of negative eigenvalues), with the top eigenvalue $1$ on $D^{1/2}\mathbf 1$ and the second eigenvalue $1-\lambda_2(L_{\rm sym})$ on the second eigenvector. Undoing the whitening, $D^{-1/2}$ times the second eigenvector of $\tilde P$ is the second eigenvector of $D^{-1}W=I-L_{\rm rw}$, which is the Fiedler vector of Shi–Malik normalised cut. Its sign gives the two-way partition, and the eigenvalue near $1$ measures how slowly the walk leaks across the cut — the same quantity as $\sigma_1$ in Problem 4(c). So normalised spectral clustering is coherent-set detection for the one-step random walk on an undirected graph.

When $W$ is asymmetric (a directed graph, or any process without detailed balance), $\tilde P$ is not symmetric: its eigenvectors need not be real or orthogonal, and its eigenvalues can be complex, so "sign of the second eigenvector" is not defined. The singular value decomposition of $\tilde P$ always exists and is real, and its left and right vectors are the two halves of a coherent pair. That replacement — eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix by singular vectors of a non-symmetric one — is the entire content of the generalisation from metastable sets (reversible, Deuflhard–Schütte) to coherent sets (Froyland 2010, 2013), and of the paper's Eqs. (6)–(9). High
